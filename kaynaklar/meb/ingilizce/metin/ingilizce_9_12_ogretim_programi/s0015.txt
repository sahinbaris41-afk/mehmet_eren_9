• “Speaking-Expression Skill”, is one of the two “Productive Skills” defined, described, and used in the 
English Language Curriculum. “Speaking-Expression Skill” is a verbal skill that learners develop while 
learning a language so as to have the competence to communicate with other people by using the 
content that includes the target sounds, grammatical items, vocabulary, and semantic expressions 
of that language effectively and meaningfully. The main elements of “Speaking-Expression Skill” are 
planning, interaction, and communication. In speaking, the learner develops the ability to achieve social 
interaction and communicative competence by using proper and meaningful content appropriate to the 
context, situation, place and time; by producing and using the correct sounds and pronunciation; by 
expressing information, thoughts and feelings correctly, meaningfully, appropriately, clearly and fluently; 
and by transferring cultural content and messages appropriately and correctly.
• The second of the “Productive Skills”, the “Writing-Expression Skill”, is the learner’s ability and capability 
to express and convey his/her own knowledge, thoughts, experiences, and feelings in a meaningful and 
written form by using the content which contains target grammatical items, vocabulary, various forms 
of self-expression, types of writing, and rules of the language. The acquisition of “Writing-Expression 
Skill”, which focuses on transfer, expression and production, begins with the “noticing-recognition-
comprehension” of relevant input in the content in the English Language Curriculum. The transfer and 
understanding of information in input is processed through the use of receptive skills such as listening, 
watching, and reading. The ability and skill to write, express, and explain involves many processes that 
require planning, implementation and rewriting (by considering the feedback given), and developing 
the text that allows the development of creativity. This skill is strengthened through different types of 
writing studies. Activities, plans, and practices in which productive skills are used in the English Language 
Curriculum, need to be carefully planned, designed, and created in a contextual and meaningful way. 
Learners need to be supported to develop their communicative competence by using their English 
“Speaking-Expression” and “Writing-Expression” skills in a natural, authentic, automatic, realistic, 
effective, efficient and meaningful way within the context.
• “Supportive Skills”, as defined and described in the English Language Curriculum, are processed with 
all the other language skills. There is a spiral, interdependent, and reciprocal relationship between 
the field skills and supportive skills therefore all the skills are used in an integrated, unified, spiral, and 
interconnected manner. Grammatical, lexical (vocabulary) and phonological aspects of a language are 
most often taken as content knowledge even if their selection and use are mentioned in the relevant 
literature without positioning and naming them in a specific way as they are related to learners’ “linguistic 
competence”. In the recognition stage of language learning, language learners encounter, perceive 
and recognise grammatical items, lexical items (vocabulary) and phonological aspects (pronunciation, 
intonation, etc.) and are expected to begin to acquire their “linguistic competence” which will later linked 
to productive skills. This recognition stage is present in the processing of “Receptive Skills”. It may 
therefore be necessary to divide the learning of grammar, vocabulary and pronunciation into two stages: 
recognition stage and selection-use stage. The “use” stage takes place in the processing of “Productive 
Skills” in language learning through the skills of “Speaking-Expression” and “Writing-Expression”.
• This determination about supportive skills in the English Language Curriculum is not for producing new 
information. It is rather for positioning the information about the selection and use of grammatical, 
lexical, and phonological aspects as “Supportive Skills” that take place in theory and practice in a vague 
way and that leads to considering how to empower learners in acquiring their “pragmatic, strategic, 
communicative, and sociolinguistic competences”.
